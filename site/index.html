Reaching a lab from anywhere

A lab is a virtual machine on a large host, and its web interfaces are ports on that host. On the lab network that is all it takes to use one. From anywhere else it takes three things more: a safe way in, a way to keep two people from driving one mesh, and a way to know which lab is which. This page explains what exists for that, how to set a host up, and what is proposed so that every lab can be reached.

A lab is a VM on a host

Each lab is one virtual machine. Inside it are the gateway and extenders of a mesh, up to a hundred Wi-Fi clients, the simulated radio medium and the services that show and drive all of it. A lab needs a host with many cores and a lot of memory, so there are few hosts and each runs one or two labs.

A lab publishes an interface in one way, the same in every lab: the host listens on a port and forwards it to the service inside the VM. The room of rdk-1001 is port 8891 in the VM and a port of its own on rev140. Which ports a lab has is written on the VM itself, so a host can be asked what its labs offer.

Three interfaces come back in most labs. The room is the live floor plan: clients move, the optimizer steers, and an operator can drag things. The controller's interface shows the mesh as the EasyMesh controller sees it. Console NG shows the radio medium: every radio, every path, read-only.

Reaching one today

On the lab network, a lab is an address and a port in a browser. Nothing asks who you are. That is convenient on a trusted network and the reason these ports must never be forwarded to the internet as they are: whoever reaches the port has the controls.

From outside, the oldest tool works. An SSH tunnel carries one port to one person who has an account on the host:

ssh -N -L 127.0.0.1:8891:<host address>:<room port> <user>@<host>
# then open http://127.0.0.1:8891/

It does not scale past the people who administer the hosts. It needs an account with a shell, one tunnel per interface, and it says nothing about who else is using the lab.

Why a lab cannot simply be shared

A mesh is shared state: one radio medium, one controller, one set of clients. Two people who load different rooms into the same lab do not get two experiments. They get one broken one. Remote access is as much about taking turns as about getting in.

The gateway

The RDK lab has had an answer since September, and it now lives in this repository. The gateway is a small service on the lab host, next to the VM and not in it. It reads the VM's published ports, puts itself in front of them, and lets a request through only if it comes from the one browser that has reserved the lab.

It is a proxy that understands what the labs' interfaces use: ordinary requests, the event streams the room sends, and WebSockets. Each interface keeps its own address and its own root path, because the interfaces were written to live at the root of a site and break when moved under a prefix. So a lab's three interfaces are three ports on one name.

The gateway has its own accounts. Passwords are at least sixteen characters and stored salted and hashed; a session is a cookie the browser cannot read from a script, and the lab's own services never see it.

Private first, public on purpose

The gateway listens only on the host itself. What carries it outward is Tailscale, in one of two modes.

Private (Serve)Public (Funnel)
Who can connectdevices on the private network that its policy allowsanyone on the internet
The visitor needsTailscale, signed ina browser
Gateway loginrequiredrequired
Reservationrequiredrequired
Switched on bypublish --mode privatepublish --mode public --confirm-public

Private is the default. Public takes a second flag, because Funnel itself authenticates nobody: the gateway's login is then the only lock. In both modes the network's identity is not trusted as a login. An anonymous visitor to a public gateway sees a sign-in page and nothing of the lab, not even who is using it.

Nothing is forwarded on a router in either mode. The host makes an outgoing connection, so a lab behind any home or office network can be published.

One operator at a time

Signing in gives nothing yet. A visitor presses Reserve lab, and from then on that one browser owns the lab: all three interfaces, their APIs and their streams. Everyone else who signs in sees who holds it and how long they have left.

The details are where such a scheme succeeds or fails.

  • Only real input counts as activity. A page that polls for metrics, an open stream or a room left playing does not keep a reservation alive. A person watching a long demonstration presses Keep session active.
  • The hard limit cannot be extended. Nobody holds a lab for a day by wiggling a mouse.
  • The end is enforced on the server. When a reservation ends, requests are refused and streams that were already open are closed within about a second.
  • A local operator comes first. Before granting a reservation the gateway asks the room whether someone on the lab network is already driving it, and refuses if so.
  • It survives a restart. The reservation and its deadlines are kept in a small database, so restarting the gateway hands the lab to nobody else.

There is no queue and no takeover. The reservation is access control. It makes no attempt to run two experiments in one mesh.

Closing the side doors

A login in front of a lab is worth little while the lab's ports are still open next to it. Publishing a lab therefore also switches on a firewall that drops traffic from the network to that lab's three ports, on the host and on the VM's own addresses. It touches nothing else: SSH, other labs and the lab's radio traffic are unaffected. Ordinary bookmarks to those ports on the lab network stop working, by design; the gateway is the way in.

The people who administer the host can still go around it, and for them there is maintenance mode. It ends any remote reservation, closes its streams and blocks new ones until it is switched off, so a test suite can run on the host without a remote visitor in the same mesh.

What it is, and is not

A gateway for a small circle of trusted collaborators. Login attempts are limited, sessions expire, the audit log records who reserved and when. It is not a hosting platform for strangers, and anyone with a shell on the host is trusted completely.

Setting a host up

On the lab host, with a lab already running. Nothing here rebuilds or restarts the lab.

sudo bash gateway/install-host.sh      # packages, a service account, the systemd units
sudo tailscale up                      # join the private network

REMOTE=/opt/easymesh-remote/manage.py
sudo $REMOTE --lab rdk-1001 configure  # find the lab's published ports; exposes nothing
sudo $REMOTE --lab rdk-1001 add-user alice
sudo $REMOTE --lab rdk-1001 publish --mode private
sudo $REMOTE --lab rdk-1001 status     # the three addresses to open

Then open the portal, /_remote/ on any of the three addresses, sign in and reserve. Before making a lab public, check it with two browsers and two accounts: the second must see "in use" and reach nothing.

The setup guide has every step, the timeouts, the firewall's scope, the checks to run before publishing and how to undo each of them.

From here on: proposals

Everything above exists and is tested. Everything below is proposed, and none of it is built.

One address per lab

Today a lab is reached as its host's name. If each lab has a name of its own on the private network, three things follow at once: a host can publish every lab it runs, sharing a name with someone shares exactly one lab, and a lab can have as many ports as it has interfaces.

https://rdk-1001.<network>/            the controller's interface
https://rdk-1001.<network>:8443/       Console NG
https://rdk-1001.<network>:10000/      the room

A lab's name already says what it is (rdk, prpl, rdk-emosa) and when it was built (1001). Making that name the address means an address identifies a lab configuration without a table to look it up in. How to give a lab its own name with the least added to a host is the first experiment.

Choosing a lab

Someone who arrives needs to see what there is: which labs, what each one is, whether it is running, whether it is free. Most of that is already known to the host. The ports are on the VM, the state is the hypervisor's, and who holds a lab is the gateway's. What is missing is a few lines of description, which each lab's build would write onto its VM as a lab card: its configuration, a title, the date and the commits it was built from.

The public poster stays the description of the labs. The directory would be the live one, and private.

Driving and watching

One operator per lab is right and should stay. What is missing around it:

AdditionWhat it is
Observersomeone who may look at every interface and change nothing; many at once, next to the one operator
Queue"next: bob", visible to both; the lab passes on after the handoff
More labsa second person who wants to drive gets another lab; the directory may start a stopped one when the host has room
Suitesa test suite takes the same reservation under its own name, so people see "the suite is running" instead of a lab that behaves oddly

Observers are the most useful and the least trivial. The gateway must know which requests change something. For the room that is clear from the request and the room's own lease; for the controller's interface it needs a list.

One window

A lab is understood by looking at several interfaces at once: the room to see where a client is, the controller to see what the mesh believes, the console to see what the radio medium did. Today that is three browser tabs. The proposal is a workspace: one page that frames the lab's interfaces as tiles.

  • Tiles can be moved, resized, zoomed and closed. A layout is remembered per kind of lab and can be passed on as a link.
  • Each interface stays where it is, on its own port. The workspace frames it and does not rewrite it. The gateway already decides who may frame what it proxies.
  • The moving and resizing is the work of an existing tiling library, not of new code.

Two alternatives are worth knowing. A ready-made dashboard of the kind people run for a home network gives a list of services and framed tiles with no code, but knows nothing of reservations. And a browser that runs on the host and is streamed to the visitor makes every interface work unchanged and lets several people watch the same screen, at the price of video delay and no access for a program.

Beyond this lies a different thing: an optimizer that runs on a developer's own machine and drives a lab through an API. That is the umbrella's proposal EasyMesh labs as a service, and it stands on what is described here.

The reasoning, the alternatives that were weighed and the experiments that come first are in the proposal.
